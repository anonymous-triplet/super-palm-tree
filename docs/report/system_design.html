1. What the system is, and the principles behind it

City Ground Zero is a live smart-city simulator: a 3D city with traffic, emergency services, utilities and a society of citizens, instrumented with sensors and actuators that an operator, or any external program, can watch and control. It is not a single simulation but a federation of specialised simulators and services that share one world. Five design principles explain almost every choice in the system:

  • Use the right engine for each job. Road traffic is hard to get right (car following, lane changes, signals, right of way), so it is delegated to SUMO, a validated traffic simulator. The game engine (Godot) draws the world and hosts everything that is spatial or visual. Neither tries to do the other’s work.
  • One owner per fact. Every piece of state (a car’s position, a citizen’s wage, a reservoir’s level) has exactly one authoritative owner. Other parts hold read-only views or ask the owner to change it. This removes contradictions such as a car drawn where the simulator says it is not.
  • Presentation never creates truth. What you see (a 3D person at a bench) is a rendering of a record that exists in the simulation layer, never the other way round. A citizen does not get a job because a model happens to stand next to a prop.
  • Loose coupling through small messages. Parts run as separate programs and talk through compact local messages. Any part can be restarted, tested or replaced alone.
  • Determinism and observability. The population, agendas and work orders are reproducible and every decision carries a stated reason, so behaviour can be explained, replayed and tested.

2. The city plan and zones

The city is an 8 × 8 grid of districts (64 blocks) with 81 junctions and 144 road links, 49 of them signal-controlled. Blocks are zoned as downtown (skyscrapers, hotels, commerce), residential (apartments and houses), commercial and industrial (workplaces and shops), parks (six, with irrigation) and public services (seven hospitals, police, emergency stations, six reservoir compounds and two renewable energy hubs). 224 buildings, kerb-aligned street furniture and a ring of overhead power lines complete the plan. The zoning is not decoration: it sets each block’s water and power demand, where citizens live and work, and where traffic and incidents concentrate.

Direction of flow. Traffic truth flows out of SUMO into Godot continuously; intent flows back only as small commands. Everything else (citizens, utilities, devices) lives inside Godot and moves by direct reads of the owner’s state, which is faster and simpler than messaging within one process.

5. Time and synchronisation

A simulation made of several engines needs one notion of “now”. In City Ground Zero SUMO is the master clock for the physical world. Each wall-clock tick the bridge runs as many SUMO steps as the chosen speed demands (1–8×), executing every step of traffic and incident logic, and publishes only the last, so accelerated runs are never less correct, just coalesced for display. Godot follows: if SUMO cannot deliver the requested speed, the engine’s own time scale is eased down to the rate actually delivered, so people, crews, timers and the clock never run ahead of the traffic. Pausing is a command to the bridge, and the pause state is echoed back so every other part freezes together.

On top of physical time sits the city clock: a day/night cycle whose pace the operator can set (from real time up to a day in minutes). The environment system owns it and drives lighting and weather; the society kernel advances its own “society minute” from the same clock, and when an operator jumps to a new time of day the change is pushed into the society so agendas, shifts and lighting stay consistent. This separation (physical time for movement, city time for lives) lets the operator fast-forward a day without breaking traffic.

7. Traffic and incidents: how an emergency changes the road

Traffic. About 200 vehicles circulate as a closed fleet: they enter at the perimeter and are rerouted legally forever, there is no teleporting and no invisible traffic that rendered cars could queue behind. About 68 of them are drawn in detail at a time. Vehicle classes (car, taxi, bus, police, ambulance, fire, tow, utility) are typed in the packet so each is drawn with the right body. Weather feeds back into behaviour: rain and storm lower a speed factor that SUMO applies to every driver.

Why the incident engine lives next to SUMO. An incident must physically change traffic, and only the traffic engine can do that. The engine therefore runs inside the bridge with direct access to SUMO: it choreographs a crash (approach, impact, slide, rest pose), closes lanes, grants controlled overtaking to queued cars, puts nearby junctions under a two-phase police program, reroutes traffic, and drives the emergency fleet. Godot then presents what it is told. There are eleven incident types: rear-end, red-light T-bone, unsafe overtake, secondary collision, breakdown, vehicle fire, lost load, building fire, water-main break, signal failure and garbage collection.

Emergency lifecycle. Each unit is always in exactly one state: stationed in its depot bay, dispatched, responding (lights on, permitted to take priority), on scene, then returning. Units are keyed by a stable unit id rather than by their SUMO vehicle, so they can pull out of a bay, drive on the real road network and park again without appearing or vanishing. On scene, presenter crews secure the area, treat people or fight the fire, and tow trucks clear wrecks only after the scene is secured. A recorder captures the published states so an incident can be replayed in a separate render layer that never touches live objects.

8. The society: separating life from appearance

The society kernel simulates 200 citizens in 80 households, employed by 13 institutions, joined by a graph of about 440 relationships, all created deterministically so a run can be reproduced. Only 32 of them have a 3D body at any moment, but all of them live: their money, fatigue, jobs and agendas change whether or not they are on screen. The separation between the kernel (truth) and the embodiment layer (a body that carries out the truth) is the most important structural decision on the people side.

  • Daily rhythm. Each citizen has an agenda built from age and employment: sleep, household tasks, commute, work or school, meals, errands and leisure, return home. The activity at any moment is derived from the simulation clock, with the reason recorded. At day close wages and expenses are applied and agendas roll forward.
  • Work orders. Registered places in the world (a meter cabinet, hydrant, bin, planter) become institutional assets with a condition and a capacity. When an asset needs service the institution opens an order; a compatible, employed, free citizen is assigned with an exclusive reservation of the place. The body walks there over a collision-checked sidewalk graph, performs the work, and reports back; only then does the kernel close the order, change the asset’s condition and the institution’s budget, pay the citizen and choose the next assignment.
  • Needs and social life. Thirst, hunger, energy and boredom rise over time and send people to fountains, vending machines, benches or the coffee cart between jobs; afterwards litter goes into the nearest bin that has room. People who meet may stop and talk, and a completed social event strengthens the relationship edge between them.
  • Trips and traffic feedback. Every movement is a trip record (origin, destination, progress, status). Once a second the kernel stores a snapshot of the live traffic context, labelled clearly as live or as a local fallback, so citizens’ explanations can refer to real conditions without pretending to map one person to one SUMO car.
  • Advice without authority. The optional language-model planner can suggest, but cannot move bodies, change employment or complete an order.

9. Utilities: demand, supply and outages

The utility system models a 42,000 kWh power reserve and six reservoirs. For each of the 64 blocks it computes demand from the actual building mix (a hospital, hotel or skyscraper consumes far more than a house), assigns the block to its nearest reservoir, and groups blocks into service zones that share meters and can be switched as a unit. Stocks fall with simulated time at the rate of demand, and a bookkeeping pass about five times a second updates meters, outages and the city HUD. Outages come from two sources: automatic load-shedding when supply is short, and operator cuts of a zone’s power or water. Their effects propagate through ordinary data rather than special cases: a cut zone’s meters read zero and report “supply offline”, its blocks are flagged and highlighted, and lights and sensors that depend on it change on the next sample. The power network is a graph of sources, feeders and three switch cabinets; opening a switch re-evaluates which consumers are still powered.

10. Sensors, actuators and the command path

All 566 devices are registered in one place and read from the live scene on demand: a traffic radar counts the simulated vehicles near it, an air monitor reflects exhaust and rain, a weight sensor on a bin reads what citizens actually threw in. The control room’s Sensors tab and every external client therefore see the same numbers. Commands follow the same path as the operator’s buttons, which is why an external command and a click are indistinguishable: a request is validated against the device’s allowed commands, then routed to the owner of that fact.

11. Control room and terminal

The control room is a single window containing the live 3D viewport and six tabs: Control (time, weather, speed, camera, signals), Incidents, Assets (highlight any class of object in the city), Utilities, Society and Sensors. It is a view and a remote control: it reads public state from the owners and issues the same commands any other client could, which keeps it from becoming a second source of truth. The MCP terminal that opens beside it is deliberately just another client of the same interface, with no special access.
